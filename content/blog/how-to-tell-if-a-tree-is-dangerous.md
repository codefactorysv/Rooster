Most trees that fail give you warning first. The problem is that the warnings
are easy to miss, and the ones that matter most are not in the canopy where
everybody looks — they are at the trunk and the base.

Here is what we actually check when we walk a property, in the order we check
it.

## 1. Look at the trunk first {#step-1}

The trunk carries everything above it, so this is where a problem is most
expensive to ignore. Walk the full circle of the tree and look for:

- **Splits and deep vertical cracks**, especially where the trunk forks
- **Hollows or cavities** — put a hand in if you can reach; healthy wood is
  firm, failing wood crumbles
- **Missing bark** in patches, with no new growth around the edge
- **Soft or spongy wood** anywhere along the lower trunk

A tree can carry a full green canopy while the trunk underneath it is hollow.
That is the single most common thing homeowners are surprised by.

## 2. Check the base and the soil {#step-2}

Look down. Two things matter here:

**Fungus.** Mushrooms or shelf fungus growing at the base of the trunk are
feeding on wood that is already breaking down. It is a symptom, not the cause,
and the cause is usually rot in the roots or the lower trunk.

**Disturbed soil.** If the ground on one side of the tree is cracked, lifting
or mounded, the root plate is moving. That is one of the clearest signals you
can get, and it is the one that most often shows up shortly before a tree goes
over.

## 3. Judge the lean — and whether it is new {#step-3}

A lean by itself is not an emergency. Trees lean toward light, away from
buildings, and away from prevailing wind, and many have done so safely for
decades.

What matters is **change**. Compare the tree against something fixed — a fence
line, a roof edge, an old photo on your phone. If the lean is new, or if it
increased after a storm, treat it as urgent rather than something to watch.

## 4. Scan the canopy for deadwood {#step-4}

Now look up:

- Bare limbs with no leaves during the growing season
- **Hangers** — limbs that have already snapped and are caught in the branches
  above, waiting for wind
- Branches growing over a roof, a driveway, a fence or a power line

A hanger is the one to act on quickly. It has already failed; the only
question left is where it lands.

## 5. Note what is underneath {#step-5}

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that sets the priority.

The exact same defect is a routine job over an open back lawn and a serious
problem over a bedroom, a car or a walkway. Before you call anyone, write down
what a failure would land on. It changes the answer more than the defect
itself does.
