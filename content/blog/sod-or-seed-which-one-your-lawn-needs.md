Both work. They fail for different reasons, and picking the wrong one for your
yard is the usual cause.

Here is the short version:

**Sod** is a finished lawn delivered in pieces. It is green the day it goes
down, it holds soil in place immediately, and it roots fast. It costs more.

**Seed** is cheaper per square foot and gives you more grass-type options. It
also asks for patience and consistent watering, and it will wash out of a
slope before it establishes.

## When sod is the right call

Choose sod when:

- **You want the yard usable soon.** Sod is walkable within weeks; seed is a
  season.
- **There is any slope.** Seed washes downhill. Sod holds from the moment it
  is laid.
- **The area gets traffic.** Kids, dogs and a front walk will not let seed
  establish.
- **You are fixing bare or patchy ground.** Sod gives an even result instead
  of a thin patchwork over old damage.
- **Curb appeal matters now** — a sale, a rental turnover, a finished
  landscaping job.

## When seed makes more sense

Choose seed when the area is large and open, the budget matters more than the
timeline, nothing will be walking on it for a while, and you can water it
consistently for weeks.

## The part that decides the outcome

Whichever you pick, the ground underneath decides whether it takes. This is
where lawns are actually won and lost.

### 1. Clear what is already there {#step-1}

Strip the dead turf, weeds and debris. Sod laid over old dead grass roots into
a layer that is already failing.

### 2. Deal with the reason it failed {#step-2}

This is the step people skip, and it is why some yards get re-sodded twice.

Find out why the old lawn died. Deep shade, standing water, compacted soil,
buried construction debris, tree roots close to the surface — every one of
those will kill new sod exactly the way it killed the last lawn, and none of
them are fixed by laying fresh turf over the top.

If the culprit is a tree casting heavy shade or roots running through the
surface, that is worth sorting out first — it is often a
[trimming or removal question](/#services) rather than a lawn question.

### 3. Grade the surface {#step-3}

Level the area and set the fall so water runs away from the house. Fix low
spots now; once sod is down, they are puddles you cannot regrade without
lifting it.

### 4. Loosen and top the soil {#step-4}

Break up compaction and add topsoil so new roots have somewhere to go instead
of sitting on a hard pan.

### 5. Lay the sod tight {#step-5}

Pieces go down staggered, like brickwork, pressed together with no gaps. Gaps
dry out at the edges, and that is exactly where a new lawn browns first.

### 6. Water it properly from day one {#step-6}

New sod has to stay consistently damp while it roots. This is the single most
common reason a sod job fails, and it is the part that happens after we leave
— so it is worth being clear about before we start.

## Then keep it

A new lawn is not finished the day it is laid. Mowing at the right height,
clean edging and fresh [mulch in the beds](/#services) are what keep it
looking like the day it went down.
